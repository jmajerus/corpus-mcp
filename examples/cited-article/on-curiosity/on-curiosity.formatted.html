On Curiosity

Curiosity has a mixed reputation. We praise it in children and scientists, and we warn against it in nearly every old story we tell. The cat it killed is proverbial. Behind the warnings and the praise is one urge, the wish to know what we don’t yet know. What changes is where we point it, and what we expect it to give us.

The fairy tales remember the warning. In the Grimms’ “Our Lady’s Child,” a poor woodcutter’s daughter is raised in heaven. At fourteen she is given the keys to its thirteen doors and told she may open twelve. She opens them one a day, and behind each sits an Apostle in a great light. Then:

Then the forbidden door alone remained, and she felt a great desire to know what could be hidden behind it, and said to the angels, “I will not quite open it, and I will not go inside it, but I will unlock it so that we can just see a little through the opening.”1 (Grimm 2012, chap. 3)

What makes the scene true is the bargaining. She doesn’t want to go in. She only wants to look, and only a little. Twelve rooms full of glory haven’t satisfied her, because the thirteenth is the only one she hasn’t seen. The story treats this as a moral test, and she fails it. But it also describes something we all recognise: curiosity is drawn most strongly by whatever is withheld, and the excuse it offers is always that this one look will be the last.

Thich Nhat Hanh, writing about love and loneliness, starts from the same urge but finds something gentler at its root:

EACH OF US HAS A DEEP DESIRE TO know and understand the world and to be known and understood in return. That is a deep natural thirst. But often this thirst causes us to wait for something outside of ourselves.2 (Hanh 2007, chap. 4)

Two things stand out. The first is that the desire runs both ways: to know is bound up with being known. Curiosity is not only how we take in the world. It is part of how we reach toward other people. The second is the warning in the last sentence, which is close to the Grimms’ warning but not the same. The danger isn’t that we’ll open the wrong door. It’s that we’ll wait at doors, expecting something outside us to satisfy a thirst it can’t. Hanh’s own examples are the phone and the inbox: checking, again, in case there is something new.

So curiosity can be a temptation and it can be a distraction. Can it also be a discipline? Diana Montalion, writing about how people learn to think in systems, takes Einstein’s line as a description of her colleagues:

“I am only passionately curious.” This describes many of the people I’ve worked with in tech. We are curious about different things, but generally speaking, the industry attracts curious people.3 (Montalion 2024, 211)

Here curiosity isn’t a moral danger or a hunger to be redirected. It’s what people do for a living. The curious are drawn to work that rewards asking how things fit together. Montalion’s point, in the pages that follow, is that this disposition has to be practised, and it can be educated out of us. We learn that passing the test matters more than understanding the subject, and the habit of asking fades.

Put the three passages side by side and a single picture appears. The girl at the thirteenth door, the person refreshing a screen, and the engineer following a question down to its cause share the same urge. What separates them is whether curiosity is aimed at what is forbidden, at what is merely new, or at understanding. The first two are both kinds of waiting, for the door to open or the message to arrive. The third is a kind of work.

That suggests a modest answer to the old warnings. Curiosity is not dangerous because it wants to know. It becomes dangerous, or merely empty, when it wants a glimpse instead of an understanding. The cure is not to suppress it, but to give it something worth knowing, and the patience to stay with it once the door is open.
